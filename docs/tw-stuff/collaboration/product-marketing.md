Technical writers and product marketers have the same goal, but they look at it from different sides. The writer helps people **use** the product. The marketer helps people **understand the product and select it**. The product marketer owns the story: **who the product is for and why it is important**. The writer owns **the how**: the setup steps, the requirements, and the limits.

Product marketing is between the product, marketing, and sales teams. Product marketers decide how to present the product. They write the launch messages and make the content for the sales team. If your company has a product marketer, this person is frequently your best partner for the product story and the names.

## What writers need from product marketing

Ask for these inputs. The first three prevent the most rework:

- **Positioning and messaging document** — who the product is for, the main benefits, and the phrases to use or not use. This document sets the tone for introductions and overviews.
- **Approved names** — the names of the product, features, and plans. Each name has rules for capital letters and trademarks.
- **Launch plan and timeline** — the dates, the features in the release, the public and internal content, and the embargoes.
- **Audience personas** — the role of the reader, their level of experience, and their goals.
- **Competitive context** — this data is not for the docs. It shows the products that users compare with your product. It also shows the questions that users ask.
- **Customer research** — interview notes, survey results, and usual objections from customers.
- **Draft announcements** — these make sure that the release notes agree with the messages to customers.
- **Brand voice guidelines** — the tone, the preferred words, and the rules for external content.

## What product marketing needs from writers

Product marketing usually asks you for this content:

- Correct and current feature docs. Marketing adds links to these docs in launch emails and pages.
- Release notes and "what's new" content.
- Getting-started guides and tutorials. Marketing sends prospects (possible customers) to these pages.
- A technical review of marketing claims.
- Data from support and doc analytics about the parts of the product that users do not understand.

The two teams also give each other information for the messages. You see where users have problems. This information comes from support tickets, search queries, and doc feedback. Marketers know the questions that customers ask before they buy. These questions tell you what to put in a getting-started guide.

Some content goes in both directions. For example, a docs tutorial becomes a blog post. Or, a marketing one-pager becomes a feature overview.

## Work in each launch phase

The two teams must work together in each phase of a launch. In each phase, work goes in both directions. A shared tool makes the work easier:

| Phase | Product marketing | Technical writer | Shared tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| Planning | Shares the messaging framework and the launch plan before a person writes copy | Makes a glossary of approved terms. For each term, writes where to use it: in the UI, in the docs, or in marketing. | Shared glossary |
| Pre-launch | Writes the landing page and the emails | Reviews them as a technical editor. Compares each number and each claim with the product. | Comments on the exact sentence in a shared document |
| Launch | Starts the campaign at the same time as the docs | Publishes the guide, the quick-start, and the release notes | Links in two directions between the landing page and the docs |
| Post-launch | Reviews traffic and leads. Shares the page analytics. | Updates the docs with data from analytics and support | Slack channel for small fixes |

These examples show what each phase looks like:

- **Planning** — users see a problem when marketing says "workspace" and the docs say "project."
- **Pre-launch** — absolute words such as "any," "all," and "instantly" need proof. Ask about claims such as "no developer needed." Is this true for all setups, or only for the default setup?
- **Launch** — the "Learn more" link on the landing page opens the setup guide. The overview in the docs has a link back to the feature page.
- **Post-launch** — sometimes a feature page has many visits, but the related doc page has few. Then the link to the doc page is possibly difficult to find.

## Usual problems

Marketing wants positive copy that starts with the benefits. Docs must be accurate and complete, and they must also show the limits. Marketing finds your jargon. You find their [claims that are not true](./sales.md#prevent-claims-that-are-not-true), such as "works with any database." A shared glossary prevents most of these problems. A shared style guide that includes the glossary prevents more, because it also covers tone and claims.

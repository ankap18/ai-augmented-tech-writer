Marketing speaks to many people at the same time, before a conversation starts. Sales speaks to one customer at a time, during the conversation.

A prospect (a possible customer) asks questions during a call. For example: "Does it integrate with X?" or "What are the limits?" Sometimes the salesperson cannot answer immediately. Then the docs give the answer. Solutions engineers also use the docs. They show demos and make proofs of concept.

## Marketing and sales compared

The two teams have the same goal: revenue. But they work at different points in the customer journey. Marketing finds leads and makes them interested in the product. When a lead is ready to buy, sales starts to work with that lead. In this table, marketing means the full marketing team. [Product marketing](./product-marketing.md) is one part of it.

<div className="table-equal-cols">

| Area | Marketing | Sales |
|---|---|---|
| Audience | The full market, with many unknown people | Specific prospects, with names |
| Communication | One to many, with published content | One to one, with calls, emails, and demos |
| Time | Long campaign cycles | Short deal cycles and quarterly targets |
| What they need from the writer | Content that is polished and consistent | Answers that are quick and specific |
| What they tell the writer | The terms that are popular in the market | The questions and objections that prospects have before they buy |

</div>

## How sales questions become docs

Prospects ask sales questions that the docs do not answer. You and sales work together to add these answers to the docs. The bold steps show where you work directly with sales:

1. A prospect asks a question that sales cannot answer immediately.
2. **Sales sends the question to you.** Sales also sends objections and examples from demos.
3. You add the answer to the docs.
4. **Sales uses the answer.** Salespeople send links to the docs during calls and in emails. Prospects read the docs to evaluate the product.

Do these three tasks to keep the loop working:

- **Ask sales for the frequent questions** — make a list of the questions. Make sure that the docs answer each one. If the docs do not answer a question, add the answer. Usually, this change is small.
- **Use their demos** — solutions engineers frequently make proofs of concept. Use these as examples in quick-start guides.
- **Keep one FAQ for sales in the docs** — if there is no FAQ for sales, salespeople write their own copies. These copies quickly become out of date.

## Prevent claims that are not true

Sales and marketing frequently disagree about the messages. Sometimes a salesperson explains the product with words that are not approved. Sometimes marketing makes claims that sales cannot deliver.

Sales also needs content that marketing did not make, such as a comparison sheet or a technical one-pager. Then salespeople write their own content.

You can prevent these problems in two ways:

- **Keep one page about capabilities and limits** — sales can link to this page or quote it. Then salespeople do not need to write their own claims.
- **Offer to review the content that sales writes** — compare one-pagers and battlecards with the docs before sales sends them.

For example, a battlecard is a short guide for salespeople. A battlecard for a new checkout feature can contain this instruction: "Do not say that it supports third-party payment providers." Sales can trust this instruction only if the docs clearly state the limit.

Product marketing also reviews claims before a launch. For your part in that review, see [Work in each launch phase](./product-marketing.md#work-in-each-launch-phase).
